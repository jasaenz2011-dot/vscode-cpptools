### The genre-specific integrity hazard

Genre and trope material is the **worst-provenance domain in this entire knowledge base.** The overwhelming majority of writing about tropes online is fan taxonomy, SEO writing-advice content, and craft blogging. It describes convention legitimately — practitioners really do observe these patterns — but it is not research and must never be dressed as research.

This module therefore distinguishes three things that outside sources routinely blur:

### How to read the two-column classification

Every genre in Part B has a **CONVENTION / OPTIONAL TROPE** table. This is the load-bearing structure of the module.

- **CONVENTION** = if this is absent, most readers stop recognising the story as belonging to this genre. Character Studio may *surface* conventions as options.
- **OPTIONAL TROPE** = extremely common, entirely skippable. Character Studio may **never** surface these as requirements, and **must never flag, score, count or comment on their absence.**

The convention column is *also* not a requirement. A child who picks "mystery" and writes something with no mystery in it has written a story. That is a legitimate outcome, not an error state. See §11.2 of the shared conventions: **no incorrect characters.**

# PART A — Foundations: what "genre" is, and what it is not

## A.1 — WHAT RESEARCH SAYS: genre is an action, not a container

**`A3-C01`** `[E2]` `[SNIPPET]` **Carolyn R. Miller, "Genre as Social Action,"** *Quarterly Journal of Speech* (1984), ERIC EJ299348. Retrieved characterization: Miller defines genre as **"typified social action"** and argues that **"genre must be conceived in terms of rhetorical action rather than substance or form"** — that "a rhetorically sound definition of genre must be centered not on the substance or the form of discourse but on the action it is used to accomplish," grounded in **conventionalized social motives found in recurrent situation-types.** This is described in retrieved sources as foundational for Rhetorical Genre Studies.
*Retrieved via:* [ERIC EJ299348](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ299348), [Taylor & Francis record](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00335638409383686), [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/238749675_Genre_as_Social_Action).

> **Why this claim is first.** The dominant folk model of genre — a shelf, a bin, a checklist of required ingredients — is *not* the model that genre theory uses. If Character Studio treats genre as a container with required contents, it builds a feature on a model the field explicitly argues against. Genre is better modelled as *"what kind of thing am I trying to do here"* than *"what must be in the box."*

**`A3-C02`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Genre affects children's comprehension, and the effect interacts with prior knowledge.** Retrieved description of *Children's text comprehension: Effects of genre, knowledge, and text cohesion*: **sixty-four fourth-grade students** read four texts (one high-cohesion and one low-cohesion text from each of narrative and expository genres). Reported: **children comprehended narrative better than expository text, but the advantage appeared only on global questions**; a **benefit of higher prior knowledge appeared only for the expository texts**; and **low-knowledge children were not able to make the knowledge-based inferences required by expository text.**
*Retrieved via:* [ASU Elsevier Pure record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/childrens-text-comprehension-effects-of-genre-knowledge-and-text-/), [ResearchGate PDF record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/234815571_Children's_text_comprehension_Effects_of_genre_knowledge_and_text_cohesion).
**Age mapping:** grade 4 → **B3 (ages 9–10)**.

**`A3-C03`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Text-structure instruction has a measurable effect on writing quality — and the surrounding numbers are contested.** A retrieved summary of elementary-grades writing-instruction meta-analysis reports **text structure instruction ES = 0.59 on writing quality**, alongside **strategy instruction ES = 1.02**, **adding self-regulation to strategy instruction ES = 0.50**, **creativity/imagery instruction ES = 0.70**, **transcription skills ES = 0.55**, and **grammar instruction ES = −0.41**. The same retrieval separately reported **SRSD at 1.04 (quality), 0.72 (writing elements), 0.59 (productivity)**, and for reading comprehension reported text-structure instruction at **Hedges' g = 0.25 (questions), 0.57 (summarization), 0.37 (recall), 0.38 (knowledge about text structure)**.
*Retrieved via:* [Academia.edu record of elementary writing meta-analysis](https://www.academia.edu/96478546/A_meta_analysis_of_writing_instruction_for_students_in_the_elementary_grades), [Ovid record](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jedup/fulltext/10.1037/a0029185~a-meta-analysis-of-writing-instruction-for-students-in-the), [Bogaerds-Hazenberg et al., *RRQ*, text structure & comprehension meta-analysis](https://ila.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/rrq.311), [ScienceDirect, primary-grade writing meta-analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1747938X21000312).

> **⚠️ CROSS-MODULE NUMBER CONFLICT — new data point for `A4-C21`.** Module `04` documents **three** different headline effect sizes attributed to "Graham et al. (2012)" for SRSD (1.17 / 0.68 / 1.02+0.50), plus 0.82 and a ">.85, 1.0–2.55" characterization. **This session retrieved a fourth attribution: SRSD at 1.04 quality / 0.72 elements / 0.59 productivity.** The 1.02 and 0.50 figures retrieved here *are* consistent with module `04`'s "most internally coherent reading," which is mild corroboration. **The 1.04/0.72/0.59 triple should be added to `A4-C21`'s conflict table and resolved only against the primary papers. Do not cite any of these numbers externally.**
> Note also that the **0.59** figure appears in this session's retrieval attached to *two different constructs* — text-structure instruction on writing quality, and SRSD on writing productivity. That coincidence is exactly the kind of collision that snippet-level retrieval cannot adjudicate.

**`A3-C04`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **Genre study in elementary writing instruction is an established, well-described practice built on inquiry and immersion, not on definition-delivery.** Retrieved descriptions: an inquiry approach "engages students in exploring texts so that they can **notice and name** the characteristics of each genre and construct **a working definition**"; an **immersion phase** precedes mini-lessons, "usually lasts from 2–5 days," exposes students to examples "before asking them to write within that genre," and is meant to let students "see the whole of the writing before the teacher goes in to teach it part by part"; mentor texts are central.
*Retrieved via:* [Fountas & Pinnell, Genre Study](https://www.fountasandpinnell.com/genrestudy/), [ReadWriteThink, Genre Study: A Collaborative Approach](https://www.readwritethink.org/classroom-resources/lesson-plans/genre-study-collaborative-approach), [Two Writing Teachers, immersion](https://twowritingteachers.org/2019/04/10/immersion-in-writing-workshop-is-always-worth-the-time/), [Literacy Partners, immersion](https://literacypartners.com/creating-a-community-of-readers-and-writers-through-immersion/), [Children's Literacy Initiative](https://cli.org/2017/06/09/not-forget-critical-aspect-genre-studies/).

> **The pedagogical direction of travel is child-notices-then-names.** The professional practice is *not* "here is the definition of a mystery, now write one." It is "here are mysteries; what do you notice?" A product that leads with a genre checklist is inverting the established practice.

**`A3-C05`** `[E3]` `[SNIPPET]` **Common Core narrative writing standards are genre-agnostic about *content* and specific about *technique*.** Retrieved standard text for grades 3–5: students "write narratives to develop real or imagined experiences or events using effective technique, descriptive details, and clear event sequences," with grade-level sub-standards covering **introducing a narrator and/or characters**, **organizing an event sequence that unfolds naturally**, **using dialogue and description to develop experiences and events or show the responses of characters to situations**, **temporal words and phrases to signal event order**, and at grade 5 **pacing** and **concrete words, phrases and sensory details.**
*Retrieved via:* [CCSS W.4.3](https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/W/4/3/), [CCSS Writing Grade 5](https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/W/5/), [CA CDE W.5.3 record](https://www2.cde.ca.gov/cacs/id/web/5759).

> **Note what is absent from the standards: any named fiction genre.** The standards ask for *character response to situation*, not for a wizard, a detective, or a spaceship. **No genre is a standards requirement.** This is the strongest available argument that Character Studio's genre features are enrichment, never compliance.

## A.2 — WHAT RESEARCH SAYS: convention, trope, cliché

**`A3-C06`** `[E6]` `[SNIPPET]` **The convention/trope/cliché distinction is a craft-community distinction, not a research finding.** Retrieved characterizations, all from craft and editorial sources: **genre conventions** are "story elements such as character archetypes, key events, and settings that are commonly found in a specific genre" that "define the reader's expectations"; **tropes** are "a familiar character type, plot point, setting, or writing style that has become instantly recognizable," and "are a specific way of delivering or presenting genre conventions"; **clichés** are "universally defined as overused and unoriginal," with the summary formulation **"a cliché is an overused instance of a trope."**
*Retrieved via:* [Savannah Gilbo, genre conventions vs tropes](https://www.savannahgilbo.com/blog/tropes), [Scribophile, cliché vs trope](https://www.scribophile.com/academy/cliche-vs-trope), [The Editing Company](https://theeditingco.com/blog/what-is-a-trope-vs-a-cliche), [Patricia C. Wrede, clichés and tropes](https://pcwrede.com/pcw-wp/cliches-and-tropes/).

> **Tiering honesty.** These are **`[E6]`** — coherent, widely shared craft vocabulary with no empirical grounding retrieved. The distinction is *useful*, and this module uses it, but the module must not claim research established it. Note also that even in these sources, "convention" is described normatively — "conventions need to be met in order for your story to work" — which is **exactly the framing Character Studio must not adopt with children.**

**`A3-C07`** `[E6]` `[SNIPPET]` **"Overused" is a claim about an adult market, not about a child.** Retrieved criticism of the "Chosen One" trope: it is "often listed scornfully as a lazy staple of the genre"; criticisms include "it's overdone, lacking suspense and strips characters of agency" and that destiny can function "like a narrative cheat code." Retrieved counter-position from the same result set: "despite the buzz on forums suggesting these tropes are overdone, the real story is much more nuanced"; the trope's "staying power comes from the pressure fantasy — facing expectations you didn't ask for, responsibilities you weren't ready for."
*Retrieved via:* [Reactor, The Flawed Fantasy of the Chosen One](https://reactormag.com/the-flawed-fantasy-of-the-chosen-one/), [The Novelry, subverting the Chosen One](https://www.thenovelry.com/blog/the-chosen-one), [Fantasy-Faction, In Defence of the Chosen One](https://fantasy-faction.com/2015/in-defence-of-the-chosen-one), [Marie Mullany, In Defense of Common Tropes](https://www.mariemullany.com/post/in-defense-of-common-tropes).

> **Directly product-relevant.** "Overused" means *a professional adult audience has read it many times.* A nine-year-old inventing a chosen-one story has not read it many times; they have discovered a shape. The staleness lives in the market, not in the child. **Character Studio must never import adult market fatigue as a quality judgment on a child's character.**

## A.3 — WHAT RESEARCH SAYS: the classificatory systems, and their critics

**`A3-C08`** `[E2]` `[SNIPPET]` **Propp's *Morphology of the Folktale*: 31 functions and 7 character roles.** Retrieved: Propp analysed Russian folktales and proposed **31 functions** ("basic units of action that move a folktale's plot forward... regardless of who does it or how") and **7 character types**, defined **"not by personality traits but by the functions they perform in the narrative"**: **Villain, Donor, Helper, Princess (and her Father), Dispatcher, Hero, False Hero.** Named example functions retrieved: Absentation (I), Interdiction (II), Villainy (VIII), Struggle (XVI), Victory (XVIII).
*Retrieved via:* [ResearchGate, The Thirty-One Functions in Propp's Morphology](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319293980_The_Thirty-One_Functions_in_Vladimir_Propp's_Morphology_of_the_Folktale_An_Outline_and_Recent_Trends_in_the_Applicability_of_the_Proppian_Taxonomic_Model), [Media Studies, Propp's narrative functions](https://media-studies.com/narrative-functions/), [Fiveable literary theory notes](https://fiveable.me/literary-theory-criticism/unit-2/vladimir-propp/study-guide/5YLLqKWkXDIvbxtl).
**Scope caveat retrieved with the claim:** the corpus was **Russian folktales.** Retrieved sources describe later work on "recent trends in the applicability" of the model — i.e. its generality is a live question, not a settled fact.

> **The single most useful idea in this module for character design.** Propp's roles are **functional, not psychological**. "Helper" is not a personality; it is a job in the plot. This maps cleanly onto what Character Studio needs — *what does this character do in the story* — and avoids the archetype trap described in `A3-C10`.

**`A3-C09`** `[E2]` `[SNIPPET]` **The Aarne–Thompson–Uther (ATU) tale-type index.** Retrieved: a "catalogue of folktale types used in folklore studies," standardized to "classify and compare international narratives by their recurring plot structures and motifs." Lineage retrieved: **Antti Aarne (1910, German)**, translated/revised/expanded by **Stith Thompson (1928, 1961)**, further revised by **Hans-Jörg Uther (2004)**. Each type carries a numeric code with letter variants (**e.g. ATU 510A**). Used in tandem with **Thompson's *Motif-Index of Folk-Literature* (1932)**.
*Retrieved via:* [Book Riot explainer](https://bookriot.com/aarne-thompson-index/), [Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/aarne-thompson-uther-tale-type-index-fables-fairy-tales), [American School of Madrid LibGuide, folklore indexes](https://asmadrid.libguides.com/c.php?g=684010&p=4883954), [University of Missouri LibGuide](https://libraryguides.missouri.edu/c.php?g=651166).

> ATU is evidence that **"this story shape recurs across cultures" is a documented empirical observation in folklore studies**, not just a vibe. It is *also* a European-scholarship apparatus applied to global material — see `A3-C11`.

**`A3-C10`** `[E6]` `[SNIPPET]` **Character archetype systems have serious, live critiques: unfalsifiability and Eurocentrism.** Retrieved criticisms of Jungian archetypes: "critics point to the **unfalsifiability** of Jungian concepts"; "because archetypes refer to hypothesized mental structures rather than observable phenomena, they are difficult to conclusively prove or disprove"; and, on culture, "modern scholarship has characterized archetypes as **an Eurocentric and colonialist device to level the specifics of individual cultures and their stories in the service of grand abstraction**," with "the universality of archetypes and symbols… questioned." A retrieved critical source states archetypes "function like literary tropes, not psychological mechanisms," being "vague enough to mean anything and flexible enough to mean nothing."
*Retrieved via:* [Sam Woolfe, critical analysis of Jung's archetypes](https://www.samwoolfe.com/2024/08/jungian-archetypes-collective-unconscious-unscientific.html), [Wikipedia, Jungian archetypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungian_archetypes), [Medium, 10 major criticisms of Jungian psychology](https://medium.com/@dr.victor.bodo/10-major-criticisms-of-jungian-psychology-2979d054b2f9).
**Tier note:** the critiques are `[E6]` (position pieces and encyclopedic summary, not experiments). But they are *sufficient* to bar archetype classification as a product feature — see `A3-C10` product rules and the cross-module tension in Part F.

**`A3-C11`** `[E2]` `[SNIPPET]` **Applying Western analytic categories to Indigenous and non-Western oral traditions is a named methodological problem.** Retrieved: "a key challenge involves **the tendency to impose Western analytical frameworks on non-Western narrative traditions**"; "Western frameworks often prioritise written histories rooted in imperial discourses, sidelining oral traditions"; the recommended direction is "incorporating Indigenous theoretical perspectives and decolonial critiques… and **respect the sovereignty of communities over their own narrative heritage**," with "**methodological pluralism**" and "analytical frameworks grounded in non-Western epistemologies."
*Retrieved via:* [AOU comparative analysis of folktales and oral traditions (PDF)](https://web.aou.edu.lb/research/online-journals/PublishingImages/Pages/calr-issue-16-issue1/Article%204.pdf), [University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa, Oral Traditions, Folklore and Cultural Studies](https://english.hawaii.edu/oral-traditions/), [TICI Journals, tribal oral narratives](https://www.ticijournals.org/tribal-folklore-exploring-and-understanding-the-tribal-oral-narratives/).

**`A3-C12`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **Some living narrative traditions carry access protocols that a genre picker cannot represent.** Retrieved from the Library of Congress guide and related sources: **"Many Native American stories are subject to restrictions based on seasonality, occasion, topic, teller, audience, or other criteria"**; "certain stories are considered sacred and can only be told during specific seasons or ceremonies"; "in some traditions, formal initiation or special ceremonies are required before they are recognized as storytellers"; and the practical recommendation to **"contact the tribal communities to which these stories and storytellers belong to ensure their proper usage,"** with preservation working best "when tribes control access, description, and interpretation rather than having stories extracted and repackaged."
*Retrieved via:* [Library of Congress, Indigenous storytelling guide](https://guides.loc.gov/folktales-oral-storytelling/Indigenous-storytelling), [Wikipedia, Protocols for Native American Archival Materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protocols_for_Native_American_Archival_Materials).

> **Corroborates module `02`'s `A2-C80`** (seasonal restrictions on Navajo Coyote narratives). Two independent modules retrieved the same protocol pattern from different sources. **This is the module's strongest cultural constraint: some story traditions are not a "genre option."**

**`A3-C13`** `[E2]` `[SNIPPET]` **Bishop's mirrors / windows / sliding glass doors.** Retrieved: Rudine Sims Bishop, professor emerita at Ohio State, published the essay in **1990**; books are **mirrors** (readers see themselves, affirming "cultural beliefs, social values, and self-worth"), **windows** (a view into cultures different from the reader's own), and **sliding glass doors** (readers "step into" the lived experiences of others).
*Retrieved via:* [Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/videos/meet-authors/mirrors-windows-and-sliding-glass-doors), [ALSC/ALA bibliography PDF](https://www.ala.org/sites/default/files/alsc/content/Dr.%20Bishop%20Selected%20Bibliography.pdf), [McNair & Edwards, *Journal of Literacy Research*-adjacent record, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/23813377211028256).

## A.4 — WHAT RESEARCH SAYS: children, imitation, and genre control

**`A3-C14`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Genre differentiation ability in primary grades is nascent — cross-referenced, not re-derived.** Module `01` claim **`A1-C107`** reports children in **grades K–2** performing three narrative tasks on the same stimulus (on-line narration, news report, embellished story) and producing **"very subtle distinctions between the texts,"** concluding that **"primary grade children have only nascent ability to apply their genre knowledge to school language tasks."**
*This module did not independently re-retrieve that source.* It is cited here because it is the single most consequential developmental fact for this module's product rules. See `01-developmental-storytelling-milestones.md` `A1-C107` and rule `R2.1s`.

**`A3-C15`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Children's writing is inherently intertextual; drawing on popular culture is documented behavior, not a lapse.** Retrieved: children "work from one text genre to the next, transforming media and similar storylines into multiple modes and media"; studies show "instances of intertextuality in children's writing and the ways that children position themselves as writers who draw on popular culture or social figures"; elementary students "incorporate popular culture into writing assignments, drawing on characters, setting, and plot from a range of sources through **intertextual tracings**." Retrieved critique of practice: such references "are often ignored, and even when validated, this doesn't happen systematically in ways that exploit their full potential as learning scaffolds," partly because "teachers are often unaware of the nature of students' engagement with popular culture." Retrieved conclusion: **"Teachers need to acknowledge the inherently intertextual nature of children's writing."**
*Retrieved via:* [Academia.edu, Intertextuality in preschoolers' engagement with popular culture](https://www.academia.edu/4084208/Intertextuality_in_preschoolers_engagement_with_popular_culture_implications_for_literacy_development), [Taylor & Francis, Forms of becoming: elementary students' self-positioning in writing](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19463014.2025.2510221), [Academia.edu, Creative Language Play(giarism) in the Elementary ELA Classroom](https://www.academia.edu/36050420/Creative_Language_Play_giarism_in_the_Elementary_English_Language_Arts_Classroom).

**`A3-C16`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Imitation is a documented developmental mechanism and is not opposed to creativity.** Retrieved: **imitation is described as a normal stage of writing development** (children "attempt to mimic true writing," copy words from "books, posters, and word walls," and produce "sequences of lines and curves meant to represent letters"). Separately retrieved: studies with children **ages 3 to 5** found that **more than 75% of children evidence "summative imitation,"** combining different action sequences from different models to solve novel problems — described as evidence "that imitation itself can be a pathway to creative innovation rather than merely formulaic repetition."
*Retrieved via:* [Thoughtful Learning, stages of emergent writing](https://k12.thoughtfullearning.com/teachersguide/writing-spot-assessment/stages-emergent-writing), [Brightwheel, stages of writing development](https://mybrightwheel.com/blog/stages-of-writing-development), [PMC, summative imitation in preschool children](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4585006/), [Academia.edu, Creativity of formulaic learning](https://www.academia.edu/2088765/Creativity_of_formulaic_learning_Pedagogy_of_imitation_and_repetition).
**Age mapping:** ages 3–5 → below **B1** and lower **B1**. **⚠️ The retrieved imitation-stage evidence concerns emergent writing and preschool problem-solving. It does NOT directly evidence that trope-borrowing at ages 9–13 is developmentally normal.**

> **`[UNVERIFIED]` — the claim I most wanted and could not get.** I searched for direct evidence that **formulaic/clichéd genre writing by older children (roughly B3–B5) is a normal and productive developmental stage.** Queries run: *"formulaic writing children normal developmental stage imitation before originality creativity research"*; *"children imitating genre in their own writing development elementary intertextuality popular culture."* What came back was (a) emergent-writing imitation at preschool age, (b) intertextuality research showing children *do* borrow (`A3-C15`), and (c) a craft-community argument. **The inference "therefore clichés at age 10 are a normal stage" is reasonable and is the stance this module adopts on the precautionary principle — but it is an inference, not a retrieved finding.** Product rules that depend on it are flagged.

**`A3-C17`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Genre preferences differ by gender in survey data — and the numbers should be handled with care.** Retrieved figures: **boys** enjoy adventure **81%**, humour **64%**, science fiction **57%**, horror **57%**; **girls** enjoy romance **68%**, realistic fiction dealing with relationships **65%**, mysteries **59%**, humour **51%**. *Retrieved via:* a result set including [ERIC ED541603, Children's and Young People's Reading Habits and Preferences](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED541603.pdf), [Reading Rockets, Boys and Books](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/about-reading/articles/boys-and-books), [AASL/ALA School Library Research, information and reading preferences](https://www.ala.org/sites/default/files/aasl/content/aaslpubsandjournals/slr/vol6/SLMR_InfoReadingPreferences_V6.pdf).
> **⚠️ Attribution honesty:** the search synthesis did **not** attribute the specific percentages to a specific one of these documents, and I could not open any of them. **The percentages are reported here as retrieved-but-unattributed.** Do not cite them.
> **⚠️ Interpretation warning:** these are *reading* preference surveys, not *writing* preference studies, and they measure what children currently choose in a media environment that already markets by gender. **They are not evidence of what a girl or a boy wants to write, and must never be used to bias what Character Studio offers a child.** See product rule **P-A6**.

**`A3-C18`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Genre-blended and hybrid texts are documented as engaging, and blending is normal in the children's market.** Retrieved: "when discussing **hybrid texts**, students **across all three grade levels sustained more interest** on the text topic, **used speculation more frequently**, and focused on extratextual topics more than the other two types of texts." ## A.5 — WHAT CHARACTER STUDIO SHOULD DO: foundational rules

Every rule cites claim IDs and states confidence. These rules bind Parts B–E.

**`P-A1` — Genre is a lens the child may pick up and put down, never a mode the tool enters.**
`A3-C01`, `A3-C14`. Picking "mystery" changes *which optional prompts exist in the drawer*. It must not change validation, must not add required fields, must not alter what counts as a finished character, and must not be re-asserted by the tool later ("your mystery still needs a suspect"). **Confidence: high.**

**`P-A2` — Genre selection is always optional and always reversible, with a first-class "not sure / doesn't matter" path.**
`A3-C01`, `A3-C18`. The no-genre path must be visually equal to the genre paths — not smaller, not greyed, not last. Changing or clearing genre must never discard or grey out anything the child already wrote. **Confidence: high.**

**`P-A3` — Do not build genre-adaptation features as a default for B1 (K–1); introduce as option at B2.**
`A3-C14`, and module `01` rule `R2.1s`. Asking a K–1 child to "make it spooky" or "write it like a fairy tale" will mostly not change output; that is a developmental fact, not non-compliance, and the tool must not treat the unchanged output as a failure to follow instructions. **This is a default, not a gate** (shared conventions §8): if a B1 child *asks* for genre play, they get it. **Confidence: moderate-high** (rests on a cross-module claim this module did not re-retrieve).

**`P-A4` — Never classify a child's character into an archetype, role, or trope taxonomy.**
`A3-C10`, and module `05`'s explicit exclusion of "any archetype/trope classification of the child's character" from the data model. The tool may **offer** a functional role as a prompt ("does anyone help them?"); it may not **assign** one, store one as an inferred field, display one, or aggregate one. **Confidence: high.** See Part F for how this interacts with `P-B*` scaffolds.

**`P-A5` — Where a role vocabulary is used at all, use functional language, not personality language.**
`A3-C08`. "Someone who helps them" / "someone who gets in their way" describes a job in a story. "The Mentor archetype" describes a person and imports a canon. Prefer the former everywhere. **Confidence: moderate-high** (Propp is `[E2]`; the design inference is mine).

**`P-A6` — Genre offerings must never be varied by inferred or declared gender.**
`A3-C17`. The preference data is reading-choice data of unverified attribution collected in an already-gender-marketed environment. Any personalization on this basis would manufacture the pattern it claims to observe. **Rule: the genre list is identical for every child.** **Confidence: high** — this rule is *strengthened*, not weakened, by the weakness of the evidence.

**`P-A7` — Treat borrowing from books, shows and games as normal authorship, not as a problem to detect.**
`A3-C15`, `A3-C16`. Character Studio must not run similarity detection against known franchises, must not warn a child their character resembles an existing one, and must not label anything "unoriginal." **The one exception is publication-surface naming**, which is a legal/branding question for the platform, not a creative judgment, and must be handled by platform policy rather than by creative feedback. **Confidence: high.**

**`P-A8` — Offer inquiry framing, not definition delivery.**
`A3-C04`. Where the tool explains a genre at all, the explanation is phrased as *what people often notice* rather than *what a mystery has to have*. **DESIGN DECISION:** cap any genre explanation at **two sentences**, and always pair it with an explicit "lots of great ones don't do this" clause. **Confidence: moderate** — the practice is `[E4]`; the two-sentence cap is invented.

**`P-A9` — No genre feature may be presented as standards alignment.**
`A3-C05`. The CCSS narrative standards name techniques, not genres. Educator-facing copy may say a genre activity *exercises* W.x.3 techniques (dialogue, description, character response to situation); it may not say a genre is required by any standard. **Confidence: high.**

**`P-A10` — Numbers from `A3-C03` may not leave the building.**
`A3-C03`, shared conventions §2. The text-structure and SRSD effect sizes retrieved this session conflict with module `04`'s already-conflicting set. They may inform internal prioritization only. **Confidence: high.**

## B.1 — Fantasy

### WHAT RESEARCH SAYS

**`A3-C20`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **Fantasy is defined by impossibility plus internal consistency, and children's-literature texts split it into two families.** Retrieved: fantasy "features events, settings, or characters which are outside the generally accepted realm of possibility, frequently using magic and other supernatural phenomena," with "frequent connections to mythology and popular folklore." Children's-literature textbooks distinguish **Low Fantasy** ("non-rational events and characters in a world or environment which is otherwise common") from **High Fantasy** ("settings which are completely fictional, transporting readers to an alternative world or reality"). Retrieved constraint: **"the author must convince the reader to suspend disbelief by creating an internally logical and consistent world."**
*Retrieved via:* [Appalachian State University, Evaluating Literature Genres for Children — Fantasy](https://guides.library.appstate.edu/c.php?g=1200949&p=8782646), [Appalachian State IMC, Genres: Definitions for Children's Literature](https://imc.library.appstate.edu/book-lists/genres-definitions-children%E2%80%99s-literature), [VCU Pressbooks, A Guide to Children's Literature ch.10](https://pressbooks.library.vcu.edu/childrensliterature/chapter/chapter-10-fantasy-and-science-fiction/).

**`A3-C21`** `[E2]` `[SNIPPET]` **Mendlesohn's four rhetorics of fantasy, organised by the protagonist's relationship to the fantastic.** Retrieved: Farah Mendlesohn, *Rhetorics of Fantasy*, **Wesleyan University Press, April 2008**, using "nearly two hundred examples." Four categories "arise out of **the relationship of the protagonist to the fantasy world**": **portal-quest** (the hero leaves normality and enters a fantasy world), **immersive** (entirely set within an imagined world), **intrusion** (the abnormal intrudes into the characters' reality), **liminal** (uncertainty about which is which). Retrieved: "many books combine writing in more than one of these rhetorical modes."
*Retrieved via:* [Wesleyan University Press](https://www.weslpress.org/9780819568687/rhetorics-of-fantasy/), [Wikipedia, Rhetorics of Fantasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetorics_of_Fantasy), [Fantasy-Hive, applying Mendlesohn's categories](https://fantasy-hive.co.uk/2019/07/the-4-categories-of-fantasy-applying-some-ideas-from-rhetorics-of-fantasy-by-farah-mendlesohn/).

> **Best-in-module scaffold candidate.** Mendlesohn's axis is *a question about the character*, not a question about the world: **where does my character stand relative to the magic?** That is answerable by a nine-year-old in one sentence, and it determines an enormous amount downstream.

**`A3-C22`** `[E6]` `[SNIPPET]` **"Magic needs rules and limits" is craft consensus with no retrieved empirical basis.** Retrieved from craft sources: "every aspect of a magic system should be consistent and coherent within its own ruleset"; "if anyone can use magic to do anything, it's boring — ensure magic has restrictions, limits, or consequences"; high-fantasy worldbuilding involves "a secondary world separate from Earth… a detailed internally consistent history, and often a complex and original magic system."
*Retrieved via:* [Literary Compass, high fantasy worldbuilding checklist](https://literarycompass.com/what-defines-high-fantasy-a-6-point-worldbuilding-checklist/), [Writers Write, magic systems](https://www.writerswrite.co.za/7-ways-to-create-a-spectacular-magic-system-for-your-novel/), [Dabble, build a magic system](https://www.dabblewriter.com/articles/build-a-magic-system).
> **⚠️ Explicit warning.** This is an adult-novelist norm. Applied to a child, "your magic needs rules and a cost" is a **craft opinion presented as a correctness standard**, and it is one of the easiest ways for this product to make a child feel wrong. Character Studio may offer it as *one* interesting question. It may never imply the story is broken without it.

**`A3-C23`** `[E6]` `[SNIPPET]` **Whether fantastical content helps or hinders children's learning from stories is an open empirical question, and I could not retrieve the findings.** Retrieved titles only: *The Magic School Bus dilemma: How fantasy affects children's learning from stories* (*Journal of Experimental Child Psychology* record) and a popular-science piece titled "Why kids can learn more from tales of fantasy than realism."
*Retrieved via:* [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022096521001302), [Aeon Ideas](https://aeon.co/ideas/why-kids-can-learn-more-from-tales-of-fantasy-than-realism).
> **`[UNVERIFIED]` as to direction and magnitude.** The titles point in *opposite* directions ("dilemma" vs "learn more from fantasy"). **I retrieved no findings and state none.** Searched: *"teaching children difference science fiction and fantasy 'could happen' plausible explanation genre distinction"*. Any Character Studio claim that fantasy is better or worse for learning would be fabrication.

### How characters function in fantasy

| Aspect | What the retrieved sources support |
|---|---|
| **Character's core demand** | A stance toward the impossible: does the character *belong* to the magic, *encounter* it, or *get invaded* by it (`A3-C21`)? |
| **Agency** | Not specified by the genre. High fantasy conventionally gives large-stakes agency; low fantasy conventionally gives ordinary-life agency plus an anomaly. `[E4]` |
| **Goals** | Conventionally externalised (recover, defeat, reach, restore) — this is convention, not requirement. `[E4]` |
| **Traits** | Nothing in the retrieved sources requires roundness. Module `01` `A1-C47`/`A1-C52` explicitly holds flatness is not a defect. |
| **Setting** | Either a consistent secondary world (high) or the ordinary world plus an anomaly (low). `A3-C20` |
| **Worldbuilding load** | High fantasy conventionally carries the heaviest worldbuilding expectation of any genre here. **This makes it the genre most likely to over-scaffold a child.** |

### CONVENTION vs OPTIONAL TROPE — fantasy

| CONVENTION (recognisability) | OPTIONAL TROPE (skippable, never required) |
|---|---|
| Something impossible is real inside the story `A3-C20` | A chosen one / prophecy `A3-C07` |
| The story treats that impossibility as consistent within itself `A3-C20` | A wise old mentor |
| A defined relationship between the character and the fantastic `A3-C21` | A magic system with named costs and rules `A3-C22` |
| | A quest object to retrieve |
| | Dark lord / ultimate evil |
| | Medieval-European setting furniture (castles, taverns, swords) |
| | Talking animal companion |
| | Orphan protagonist |
| | Hidden magical heritage revealed |

**Nothing in the right column may ever be requested, required, counted, or noted-as-missing.** A fantasy character who is a happy child with two living parents, no destiny, and unexplained magic is a complete fantasy character.

### Clichés, and the honest position on them

The right-column items most often called clichés in retrieved craft criticism are **the chosen one**, the **orphan**, and **the dark lord** (`A3-C07`). Character Studio's position, per `A3-C07`: *overused* is a property of an adult market. Treat all of these as neutral.

### Child-achievable subversions (fantasy)

**These are offers, never corrections.** Each is stated as a question a child can actually answer.

| Band | Subversion the child can actually execute | Why it is achievable |
|---|---|---|
| **B2 (7–8)** | "What if the magic only works on something small and silly?" | Single-swap; no plot restructuring |
| **B2–B3** | "What if your character is the helper and someone else is the hero?" | Role swap using functional vocabulary `A3-C08` |
| **B3 (9–10)** | "What if the chosen one didn't want to be chosen?" | Adds one motive; keeps the shape |
| **B3–B4** | "What if the scary creature is the one who needs help?" | Aligns with documented moral-ambiguity development `A3-C44` |
| **B4 (11–12)** | "What if the magic costs your character something they care about?" | Introduces `A3-C22`'s cost idea *as a choice* |
| **B5 (13–14)** | "What if the prophecy is wrong, or someone made it up?" | Requires holding two framings at once |

### Cultural notes (fantasy)

The high/low taxonomy (`A3-C20`) and the four-rhetorics taxonomy (`A3-C21`) were both built on **Anglophone corpora**. The "medieval European furniture" cluster in the trope column is a **regional aesthetic, not a genre requirement**, and Character Studio's example imagery must not default to it. See Part G.

## B.2 — Adventure

### WHAT RESEARCH SAYS

**`A3-C24`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **Adventure is defined by action, risk, and journey; retrieved sources explicitly note its characters are conventionally simple.** Retrieved: adventure fiction is "characterized by thrilling plots centered on exciting undertakings involving risk, physical danger, heroism, and often exploration or quests in exotic or unusual settings"; "action-driven plot lines are one of the key defining characteristics"; protagonists are "resourceful individuals… who face perilous challenges that test their courage, ingenuity, and moral fiber"; "**characters in adventure stories tend to be simple with little evolution**"; the protagonist "regularly embarks on some sort of quest, mission, voyage or expedition"; themes include "the struggle between good and evil" and "one person or a group of people against the environment."
*Retrieved via:* [Children's Adventure Genre Guide — Elements of Adventure](https://childrensadventuregenreguide.wordpress.com/elements-of-adventure/), [Children's Adventure Genre Guide — Themes and Motifs](https://childrensadventuregenreguide.wordpress.com/themes-and-motifs/), [Study.com, Adventure Fiction](https://study.com/academy/lesson/adventure-fiction-definition-list-examples.html).
> **Tiering honesty:** the most detailed source here is an unattributed genre-guide site. `[E4]` is generous; treat as **`[E4]`/`[E6]` boundary**. It is included because its description is consistent with the library-guide sources and because Character Studio needs *some* working description.

> **This is the highest-value single line in Part B for product safety:** a mainstream professional description of adventure says its characters are *conventionally simple with little evolution.* That is direct support for the shared-conventions commitment that **flat characters are not defects** (§11.2, §11.3, module `01` `A1-C47`). In adventure, flatness is the *house style*.

### How characters function in adventure

Character exists to *meet obstacles*. What the genre asks of a character is **capability under pressure and a reason to keep going** — not interiority, not change. Goals are external and concrete. Conflict is character-vs-environment and character-vs-antagonist. Worldbuilding load is **low**: the setting must be dangerous and traversable, not systematised.

### CONVENTION vs OPTIONAL TROPE — adventure

| CONVENTION | OPTIONAL TROPE |
|---|---|
| The character goes somewhere or does something risky `A3-C24` | A treasure or prize |
| Physical or situational danger is real inside the story `A3-C24` | A map |
| Forward momentum — events keep happening `A3-C24` | Absent/unavailable adults |
| | A rival who becomes an ally |
| | A ticking clock |
| | Exotic or "far away" locale — see cultural note |
| | A loyal animal |

### Clichés (adventure)

The one that carries real cultural weight is the **"exotic setting"** convention (`A3-C24` uses that exact word). In children's adventure this has a long history of rendering non-Western places as scenery and their inhabitants as obstacles. **Character Studio must not use the word "exotic" anywhere, and must not offer "a faraway jungle/island/desert full of dangers" as a setting suggestion.** *(This is a product judgment informed by `A3-C11` and `A3-C13`, not a retrieved finding.)*

### Child-achievable subversions (adventure)

| Band | Subversion |
|---|---|
| **B2** | "What if the dangerous place is somewhere really ordinary?" (the basement, the bus) |
| **B3** | "What if your character is scared the whole time and goes anyway?" |
| **B3–B4** | "What if the group only survives because of the character everyone underestimated?" |
| **B4** | "What if the treasure isn't worth it?" |
| **B5** | "What if the 'wild' place is somebody's home, and your character is the intruder?" |

## B.3 — Mystery

### WHAT RESEARCH SAYS

**`A3-C25`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **Mystery has the most explicit, most teachable element vocabulary of any genre in this module, and it is embedded in mainstream literacy curriculum.** Retrieved element list: **detective, sleuth, witness, suspect, clues, red herring, case, crime, victim, alibi, evidence, hunch, motive, suspense, breakthrough, deduction, criminal.** Retrieved structure: mysteries "start with an exposition of the problem, clues that may help the reader guess the ending, and the end in which the solution is exposed"; the protagonist "gathers clues, interviews witnesses, examines evidence, and analyzes the facts." Retrieved pedagogy: teachers "have students identify the characteristics of mystery writing in class discussions, outline a mystery story using a graphic organizer, write and revise a mystery story on their own, and edit each other's work."
*Retrieved via:* [ReadWriteThink, What's in a Mystery?](https://www.readwritethink.org/classroom-resources/lesson-plans/what-mystery-exploring-identifying), [ReadWriteThink, Investigating Genre: The Case of the Classic Detective Story](https://www.readwritethink.org/classroom-resources/lesson-plans/investigating-genre-case-classic), [ReadWriteThink, Everyone Loves a Mystery: A Genre Study](https://www.readwritethink.org/classroom-resources/lesson-plans/everyone-loves-mystery-genre), [Mystery Writers of America Educator's Page](https://mysterywriters.org/mwa-literacy-programs/educators-page/).

**`A3-C26`** `[E2]` `[SNIPPET]` **"Fair play" is a codified historical convention of the detective genre.** Retrieved: during the Golden Age, writers set up rules "to ensure 'fair play' for the readers"; **Ronald Knox codified ten rules in 1929** ("Knox's Decalogue"), including "the criminal must be mentioned in the early part of the story, but must not be anyone whose thoughts the reader has been allowed to know" and "all supernatural or preternatural agencies are ruled out"; **S. S. Van Dine, "Twenty Rules for Writing Detective Stories," *The American Magazine*, September 1928**, holding that "the reader must have equal opportunity with the detective for solving the mystery" and "all clues must be plainly stated and described."
*Retrieved via:* [No Film School, Knox's 10 commandments](https://nofilmschool.com/ronald-knox-detective-fiction-commandments), [Elizabeth Spann Craig, Ten Commandments for Detective Fiction](https://elizabethspanncraig.com/mystery-writing-tips/ten-commandments-detective-fiction-1929-brief-history/), [Agatha Christie official site, Playing by the Rules](https://www.agathachristie.com/news/2019/playing-by-the-rules-christies-unconventional-crimes), [Cozy-Mystery.com, Knox's Commandments](https://cozy-mystery.com/blog/knoxs-commandments-the-10-rules-of-golden-age-detective-fiction-part-ii/).
> **Note the retrieved fact that Christie herself is discussed under "unconventional crimes" — i.e. the canonical author of the genre broke its codified rules.** That is a *usable* fact for a child: **the rules were made up by fans of the genre, and the most famous mystery writer broke them.**

### How characters function in mystery

Mystery is the genre with the **clearest functional role set** after folktale. The demand on the protagonist is **noticing and reasoning** — an epistemic capability, not a physical one. This makes mystery unusually accessible to children who are not drawn to action, and it is the one genre where "what does your character notice that others don't?" is both a genre question and a characterisation question at the same time.

| Functional role | What it demands of the character |
|---|---|
| Detective/sleuth | Attention, persistence, a reason to care about this case |
| Suspect | A plausible motive and a plausible innocence |
| Witness | Partial knowledge — and a reason their account is incomplete |
| Victim | A relationship to someone in the story (or nothing — see below) |

> **Important product note:** `A3-C25`'s element list includes **crime, victim, criminal**. Character Studio serves grades 2–8. **The tool must be able to run mystery scaffolding with the crime vocabulary entirely absent** — "something is missing," "something doesn't add up," "someone isn't telling the truth" all produce mysteries without a body. See `P-D7`.

### CONVENTION vs OPTIONAL TROPE — mystery

| CONVENTION | OPTIONAL TROPE |
|---|---|
| Something is unknown, and someone wants to know it `A3-C25` | A crime, especially a violent one |
| Information reaches the reader gradually `A3-C25` | Red herrings |
| Someone does the finding-out `A3-C25` | A gathering-of-suspects reveal scene |
| | A rival detective or dismissive adult |
| | A sidekick who asks the questions |
| | The least likely suspect did it |
| | Fair-play clue disclosure `A3-C26` |

> **`A3-C26` sits in the right column deliberately.** Fair play is a *codified convention of one historical movement*, not a requirement of mystery. A child whose detective solves it by intuition has written a mystery.

### Child-achievable subversions (mystery)

| Band | Subversion |
|---|---|
| **B2** | "What if the mystery is solved by someone very small, or an animal?" |
| **B2–B3** | "What if nothing was stolen — someone just moved it?" |
| **B3** | "What if your detective is wrong at the end, and doesn't find out?" |
| **B4** | "What if the person who did it had a good reason?" (pairs with `A3-C44`) |
| **B4–B5** | "What if your detective is the one hiding something?" |
| **B5** | "What if the mystery can't be solved, and the story is about living with that?" |

## B.4 — Realistic fiction

### WHAT RESEARCH SAYS

**`A3-C27`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **Realistic fiction is defined by possibility, not by truth.** Retrieved: "contemporary realistic fiction implies that everything in a realistic story — including plot, characters, and setting — is consistent with the lives of real people in our contemporary world"; "**the word realistic does not mean that the story is true… it means only that the story could have happened**"; plots "deal with familiar, everyday problems, pleasures, and personal relationships," and "characters and settings seem as real as the contemporary world we know." Retrieved from a children's-literature guide: mystery is sometimes "covered as a subgenre within realistic fiction," showing "children resolving conflict in their lives."
*Retrieved via:* [Salve Regina University LibGuide, Realistic Fiction](https://salve.libguides.com/c.php?g=434935&p=2965212), [Appalachian State, Evaluating Literature Genres — Realistic Fiction](https://guides.library.appstate.edu/c.php?g=1200949&p=8782652), [University of Saskatchewan LibGuide](https://libguides.usask.ca/childlit/realistic_fiction), [McHenry County College LibGuide, Children's Literature Genres](https://libguides.mchenry.edu/childlit).

**`A3-C28`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **The "problem novel" is a named subcategory, and its topic list is heavy.** Retrieved: "most of the topics focus on coping with serious problems of the human condition"; "when the problem is not the focus, the book generally is not a problem novel"; for older readers realistic fiction "is often considered synonymous with social critical literature and the problem book." Retrieved topic lists include **divorce, peer pressure, growing up, living in a family, fears, school issues**, and, in a second retrieved list, **abuse, illness, death, grief, depression, parental job loss, moving, and poverty.**
*Retrieved via:* [Quizlet study set for a children's-literature course chapter](https://quizlet.com/413607937/ch-11-contemporary-realistic-fiction-flash-cards/), [Montana State Billings LibGuide](https://libguides.msubillings.edu/c.php?g=242172&p=1610211), [University of Texas Tyler LibGuide](https://libguides.uttyler.edu/c.php?g=357600&p=2413335).
> **Tier caution:** one of the three retrievals is a student-made flashcard set summarising a textbook chapter. That is a **weak** source and the claim is tiered `[E4]` only because the two library guides corroborate the general shape. Treat the topic lists as illustrative, not canonical.

> **⚠️ SAFETY-CRITICAL PRODUCT IMPLICATION — the most important line in Part B.** Realistic fiction is the genre in which a child's own life is most likely to arrive in the text. Character Studio must **never suggest** items from `A3-C28`'s topic list to a child. Offering "maybe your character's parents are getting divorced" to a nine-year-old is a product prompting a child toward disclosure. **Realistic fiction gets the *fewest* content suggestions of any genre, not the most.** See `P-D8`, and route to module `05` for the disclosure-handling and privacy posture.

### How characters function in realistic fiction

Realistic fiction makes the heaviest **interiority** demand of the genres here — the character's inner response *is* the content, because there is no dragon to externalise it onto. But note module `01` `A1-C44`: internal-state language develops over an extended period and varies by language and culture, and the tool **may offer one interiority prompt and may never report low interiority.**

Worldbuilding load: **lowest of any genre.** This makes realistic fiction the correct default for a child who is finding worldbuilding exhausting, and a good fit for several accessibility profiles (Part H).

### CONVENTION vs OPTIONAL TROPE — realistic fiction

| CONVENTION | OPTIONAL TROPE |
|---|---|
| Everything in the story could happen `A3-C27` | A serious social problem at the centre `A3-C28` |
| The world is recognisably the ordinary world `A3-C27` | A lesson learned / visible growth |
| | A school setting |
| | A friendship rupture and repair |
| | A misunderstood-by-adults dynamic |
| | A sad or bittersweet ending |

### Child-achievable subversions (realistic fiction)

| Band | Subversion |
|---|---|
| **B2** | "What if the whole story is about one small ordinary thing?" |
| **B3** | "What if nothing bad happens and it's still interesting?" |
| **B3–B4** | "What if the adult is right and your character is wrong?" |
| **B4** | "What if your character doesn't change, and that's the point?" |
| **B5** | "What if the problem isn't solved, just carried differently?" |

## B.5 — Science fiction

### WHAT RESEARCH SAYS

**`A3-C29`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **SF is conventionally separated from fantasy by *plausibility and explanation*, not by furniture.** Retrieved: "fantasy involves fictionalizing **the impossible**… science fiction involves fictionalizing **the plausible or possible**, extrapolating from what we know"; "the crazy stuff that happens in sci-fi is explained with science, while fantasy phenomena are rooted in magic"; SF features "scientifically plausible or technologically possible developments that were imaginary at the time of publication, but could occur in the future." ### How characters function in science fiction

The genre's demand is **a character positioned relative to a change** — a technology, a discovery, an altered condition — and `A3-C30`'s "how characters are affected by the science elements" is the load-bearing phrase. Crucially: SF **does not require the child to explain the science.** The convention is that an explanation *exists in the world*, not that the author supplies it.

### CONVENTION vs OPTIONAL TROPE — science fiction

| CONVENTION | OPTIONAL TROPE |
|---|---|
| Something is different from now, and the story treats it as explicable rather than magical `A3-C29` | Space, spaceships, other planets |
| The character is affected by that difference `A3-C30` | Robots or AI |
| | The future as a time setting |
| | Aliens |
| | Dystopian government |
| | A scientist character |
| | An explanation of how the technology works |

> **The last right-column row matters.** Children frequently believe SF requires them to justify the technology. It does not (`A3-C30`). **Character Studio must never ask a child how their invention works** unless the child raises it.

### Child-achievable subversions (science fiction)

| Band | Subversion |
|---|---|
| **B2** | "What if the robot is bad at its job?" |
| **B3** | "What if the amazing invention makes life *slightly* worse in an annoying way?" |
| **B3–B4** | "What if the aliens are the ones who are scared?" |
| **B4** | "What if your character is the technology?" |
| **B5** | "What if the invention works exactly as promised, and that's the problem?" |

### Cultural note (science fiction)

SF's conventional futures carry embedded assumptions about **whose future it is**. Retrieved sources on MG SF gesture at "what it means to be human" and "science and ethics" (`A3-C30`) without addressing this. Character Studio's example set must not present a single culturally-uniform future. *(Design judgment informed by `A3-C13`, not a retrieved finding.)*

## B.7 — Folktale (and why this label is the most dangerous one in the module)

### WHAT RESEARCH SAYS

### Child-achievable subversions (folktale)

Subversion is a **culturally loaded move in this category** in a way it is not for, say, adventure. Inviting a child to subvert a living tradition's story is not equivalent to inviting them to subvert the chosen-one trope.

**Rule, not a table:** Character Studio offers subversion prompts for folktale **only** for story shapes the child has themselves introduced, and never for a named tradition the tool surfaced. If the child says "I want to write an Anansi story," the tool does not respond with "what if Anansi lost?" See `P-B7c`.

### WHAT RESEARCH SAYS

*Retrieved via:* [Wikipedia, The Uses of Enchantment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Uses_of_Enchantment), [SuperSummary study guide](https://www.supersummary.com/the-uses-of-enchantment/summary/), [alicechristina.blog critical reading](https://alicechristinadotblog.wordpress.com/2021/01/25/considering-the-use-of-fairy-tales-to-heal-trauma-bettelheims-uses-of-enchantment/).

### How characters function in humor

Humor is the module's clearest case of a genre that is a **tone rather than a content category** — it combines with every other genre and is arguably always a hybrid (Part B.11). What it asks of a character is a **comic engine**: a mismatch between what the character believes and what is true, a wildly disproportionate reaction, or a plan that cannot survive contact with reality.

| Something is wrong, and the story wants you to feel it before you see it `A3-C54` | A monster, ghost, or supernatural entity |
| The character is vulnerable in some way | A haunted house or abandoned place |
| Anticipation carries the weight `A3-C54` | Night / darkness |
| | A curse or rule that must not be broken |
| | Disbelieving adults |
| | A jump scare |
| | Anyone being harmed |

> **"Anyone being harmed" is in the OPTIONAL column and this is the load-bearing claim of Part D.** A spooky story requires *dread*, not *damage* (`A3-C54`). This is what makes a genuinely scary, genuinely age-appropriate spooky mode possible.

## B.11 — Hybrids and genre blending

### WHAT RESEARCH SAYS

Grounded in **`A3-C18`** (hybrid texts sustained more interest and more speculation across grade levels; genre blends are among the most popular children's/teen books; blends let readers "expand across boundaries… while still in the safety of their preferred reading choice"). Also relevant: **`A3-C21`** — Mendlesohn's retrieved observation that "many books combine writing in more than one of these rhetorical modes," with *The Lord of the Rings* named as beginning as a quest, becoming immersive, and turning intrusive.

**Interpretation:** blending is not a deviation that the taxonomy has to tolerate. It is (a) documented as engaging, (b) the norm in the market children read, and (c) present inside the scholarly taxonomies themselves.

**`[UNVERIFIED]` — how often *children* blend when writing.** I found no study measuring genre-blending rates in children's own composition. Searched: *"genre blending hybrid genre children's books mashup writing classroom."* The premise that "children blend constantly" is **plausible, consistent with `A3-C15` (intertextuality), and not directly evidenced.** Product rules below are built to be *safe if it is true and harmless if it is not* — they add permissiveness, never requirements.

### How hybrids behave, functionally

| Blend | What conventionally dominates | The productive tension for a character |
|---|---|---|
| Fantasy + mystery | The mystery's information structure | Can magic solve it? If so, why isn't it solved? |
| SF + adventure | Adventure's momentum | Does the technology help or trap them? |
| Realistic + spooky | Realism's setting, spooky's tone | Is it real? Does the character know? |
| Fable + humor | The fable's compression | The moral fights the joke |
| Fairy tale + realistic | Fairy tale's roles, realistic's texture | An ordinary person in a role-shaped world |
| Mystery + humor | Mystery's structure | The detective is the least competent person present |

**Note the pattern:** in every row, the blend *generates* a character question. Blending is a characterisation engine, which is a good reason for a character-creation tool to support it enthusiastically.

## B.12 — WHAT CHARACTER STUDIO SHOULD DO: genre profile rules

**`P-B1` — The convention/trope split is implemented as data, not as prose.**
All of Part B. Each genre record stores two disjoint lists. **Only the CONVENTION list may ever generate a prompt.** The OPTIONAL TROPE list exists so the system can *recognise* these when a child uses them and deliberately **do nothing**. **Confidence: high.**

**`P-B2` — Absence detection is prohibited, full stop.**
Shared conventions §11.2; `A3-C24`, `A3-C31`. No code path may compute "this fantasy character has no magic," "this mystery has no suspect," "this fable has no moral." Not for feedback, not for analytics, not for educator dashboards, not internally. This aligns with module `05`'s exclusion of `completeness_percent`. **Confidence: high.**

**`P-B3` — Genre-specific suppression of roundness nudges.**
`A3-C24`, `A3-C31`, module `01` `A1-C47`/`A1-C52`. When the child's chosen genre is **fable, folktale, fairy tale, or adventure**, any prompt whose purpose is to add interiority, complexity, or change is **suppressed by default**, because flatness is the documented convention of those forms. It remains available if the child opens it. **Confidence: high** — this is the rule most directly supported by retrieved genre descriptions.

**`P-B4` — Worldbuilding prompts scale to the genre's actual load, and cap low.**
`A3-C20`, `A3-C22`, `A3-C27`, `A3-C30`. Suggested worldbuilding prompt allowance: **high fantasy ≤ 2, SF ≤ 2, low fantasy ≤ 1, adventure ≤ 1, mystery ≤ 1, fable 0, fairy tale 0, realistic fiction 0** unrequested prompts. **DESIGN DECISION** on every number. These sit **inside** module `04`'s budget of 3 unrequested offers per character — they do not add to it. See Part F. **Confidence: moderate.**

**`P-B5` — Never ask a child to explain how the magic or the technology works.**
`A3-C22`, `A3-C29`, `A3-C30`. The convention is that an explanation exists in the world, not that the author provides one. If the child volunteers a system, the tool may engage with it. **Confidence: moderate-high.**

**`P-B6` — Mystery scaffolds ship in a no-crime configuration by default.**
`A3-C25`. The vocabulary "crime / victim / criminal" is available on request but is never the framing the tool offers first. Default framings: *something is missing · something doesn't add up · someone isn't telling the truth.* **Confidence: high** (a safety judgment for a grades 2–8 product; the underlying element list is `[E4]`).

**`P-B7` — The "folktale" button must not flatten living traditions.** `A3-C11`, `A3-C12`, `A3-C34`–`A3-C37`. Three sub-rules:

- **`P-B7a` — The tool never generates content *from* a named living tradition.** It will not produce an Anansi story, a Jataka tale, a Coyote story, or a La Llorona story, and will not offer characters, plots, or motifs attributed to one. It will happily support a child who brings one. Rationale: `A3-C12`'s access protocols cannot be represented in a genre picker, and `A3-C35` shows what generic trope-carding does to Anansi.
- **`P-B7b` — Where a tradition is named at all, name it specifically and attribute it.** Not "folktales" but "stories from the Akan people of West Africa." Rationale: `A3-C11`, `A3-C35`.
- **`P-B7c` — No subversion prompts for material from a named tradition.** Subversion prompts fire only on shapes the child introduced. **Confidence: high on all three.**

**`P-B8` — Fairy-tale defaults must not reproduce the class-and-gender package.**
`A3-C38`. Princesses, wicked stepmothers, beauty-as-goodness and marriage-as-resolution stay entirely in the optional column; the tool never proposes them. **Confidence: moderate-high** (`A3-C38` is `[E2]`; the design response is mine).

**`P-B9` — Model subversion on the fractured fairy tale, not on "avoid clichés."**
`A3-C40`, `A3-C07`. The fractured-fairy-tale frame is additive ("now do a version where…") and works *because* the child already knows the original. Anti-cliché framing is subtractive and implies the child's first instinct was wrong. **Every subversion offer in this module is phrased "what if…", never "instead of…".** **Confidence: high.**

**`P-B10` — Blending is a first-class state, never an error.**
`A3-C18`, `A3-C21`. The genre control is **multi-select**. Selecting two genres shows the union of their optional prompts, deduplicated and still capped by `P-B4`. No conflict warning, no "pick one," no primary/secondary hierarchy. If the child's text drifts from the chosen genre, **nothing happens.** **Confidence: high.**

**`P-B11` — Never use the word "exotic," and never propose a generic "faraway dangerous place."**
`A3-C24` (which uses the word), `A3-C11`, `A3-C13`. **Confidence: high**; this is a design judgment, not a finding.

**`P-B12` — Humor prompts exclude ridicule and harm.**
`A3-C41`, `A3-C42`. The retrieved element list includes ridicule and violence; Character Studio surfaces incongruity, exaggeration, absurdity, surprise, and wordplay only. **DESIGN DECISION.** **Confidence: moderate.**

# PART C — Tropes, clichés, subversion, and the morality of characters

## C.1 — WHAT RESEARCH SAYS: children's moral reasoning about characters

This is the evidence that determines **which subversions are actually reachable at which band** — because most productive character subversion is a moral-complexity move ("the villain has a reason," "the hero is wrong").

**`A3-C44`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Children's ability to assess morally ambiguous characters develops measurably between 4 and 8.** Retrieved: "most research on the development of moral judgments has focused on children's assessment of all-good or all-bad characters with little attention to the gray areas of morality"; the study found **"the 6- and 8-year-olds were more likely than the 4-year-olds to distinguish ambiguous characters from moral and immoral characters,"** described as elucidating "the development of children's assessments of the 'gray areas' of morality."
*Retrieved via:* [ScienceDirect, *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*, Heroes, villains, and everything in between](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022096525000578), [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390280202_Heroes_villains_and_everything_in_between_Children's_assessment_of_morally_ambiguous_characters).
**Age mapping:** ages 4, 6, 8 → below B1, **B1**, **B2**. **Sample size not retrieved.**
> **Read the direction carefully.** The finding is that the *distinction ability* is present by 6–8. It is **not** a finding that children below some age cannot handle a sympathetic villain. Character Studio's bands start at B2 (7–8), which is at or above the age where the retrieved study found the ability present.

**`A3-C45`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Children's default categorisation of characters runs on appearance.** Retrieved: "children usually use the external and physical features of characters in movies or stories as a means of categorizing them quickly as being either good or bad/evil."
*Retrieved via:* [Frontiers in Psychology, Does It Look Good or Evil? Children's Recognition of Moral Identities in Illustrations of Characters in Stories](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.552387/full).
> **Sharply product-relevant, and uncomfortable.** If a character-creation tool offers visual appearance options, and children read appearance as moral signal, then **any appearance-to-goodness mapping in the tool's art, examples, or defaults teaches something.** Scars, dark colours, crooked features and "ugly = bad" are not neutral choices. See `P-C7`.

**`A3-C46`** `[E6]` `[SNIPPET]` **A critique exists of binary moral framing in educational narrative.** Retrieved: binary moral constructs such as "good vs. evil" and "right vs. wrong" are "frequently embedded in educational narratives," and "while intended to simplify complex topics for young learners, such dichotomous framing **can lead to cognitive rigidity, inhibit critical thinking, and foster polarized worldviews.**" Separately retrieved: children's "evaluations reveal complex perceptions of heroes and villains, often blending traits of both," and "children struggle to categorize characters as purely heroes or villains."
*Retrieved via:* [ResearchGate, Narrative Framing and Moral Absolutism](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391671986_NARRATIVE_FRAMING_AND_MORAL_ABSOLUTISM_COGNITIVE_EFFECTS_OF_BINARY_MORAL_CONSTRUCTS_IN_EDUCATION), [Academia.edu, Children's Evaluations of Heroes and Villains in Children's Literature](https://www.academia.edu/12096141/Children_s_Evaluations_of_Villains_in_Children_s_the_Heroes_and_Literature), [University of Michigan Rackham, Sympathy for the Devil: How Children See Villains](https://rackham.umich.edu/discover-rackham/sympathy-for-the-devil-how-children-see-villains/).
> **`[E6]` and used carefully.** This must **not** become a product rule that pushes children away from clear heroes and villains. Adventure's "struggle between good and evil" is a documented convention of a genre children love (`A3-C24`), and fable requires characters who embody a single quality (`A3-C31`). The correct product reading is: **moral complexity is offered, never required, and its absence is never a deficiency.**

**`A3-C47`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **Good-guy/bad-guy play has documented developmental functions.** Retrieved: "pretend play offers kids a low-stakes arena to rehearse social roles, language, and helping behaviors"; superhero play "engages their drama skills, expands their vocabulary and language development, and is a way for children to develop healthy emotions, leading to them learning to regulate their feelings"; and "when playing with superhero figures compared to more generic toy figures, boys showed **more pro-social behaviour, negotiations about pretend play, and sharing meanings** with the superhero toys." Retrieved counter-consideration: "frequent exposure and identification with superheroes are concerning because the media glorifies superheroes… promoting play situations where children may attempt superhuman abilities and hurt themselves."
*Retrieved via:* [PMC, Mini marvels: superhero engagement across early childhood](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12162708/), [ResearchGate, Superhero Toys and Boys' Physically Active and Imaginative Play](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/248909261_Superhero_Toys_and_Boys'_Physically_Active_and_Imaginative_Play), [CSU ScholarWorks thesis, Superhero Play Among Preschool Children](https://scholarworks.calstate.edu/downloads/rn3013757).
**Population:** predominantly **preschool**, below B1, and the strongest retrieved finding is **boys-only**. **Do not generalise to grades 2–8.** The physical-risk caveat concerns embodied play, not writing, and is **not** a reason to restrict superhero characters in a writing tool.

## C.2 — WHAT RESEARCH SAYS: the honest state of the cliché question

Three things are true simultaneously and must be held together:

1. **Craft communities have a coherent, useful vocabulary** distinguishing convention / trope / cliché (`A3-C06`) — with **no empirical grounding retrieved**, and with normative framing ("conventions need to be met") that is wrong for children.
2. **"Overused" describes an adult market's reading history**, not a child's invention (`A3-C07`), and even within craft communities the "overused" verdict is contested.
3. **Borrowing is documented, normal, and under-exploited as a scaffold** (`A3-C15`); imitation is a documented mechanism that can lead to novel combination (`A3-C16`).

**What I could not establish** — and this is the module's largest single evidence gap — is a direct empirical finding that **clichéd genre writing in the B3–B5 range is a normal and necessary developmental stage.** See the `[UNVERIFIED]` note attached to `A3-C16`.

**Given that gap, shared conventions §9 governs: thin evidence ⇒ neutrality or gentle optionality, never prescription.** The product stance below is therefore *maximally permissive about clichés*, which is also the stance that is safe if the gap were later filled in either direction.

## C.3 — WHAT CHARACTER STUDIO SHOULD DO: trope, cliché and subversion rules

**`P-C1` — Character Studio has no concept of a cliché.**
`A3-C07`, `A3-C15`, `A3-C16`, shared conventions §9 and §11.2. There is no cliché detector, no originality score, no "this is a common choice" hint, no de-prioritisation of common choices in any ranking, and no differently-worded response to a chosen-one character than to any other. **Confidence: high.** *This is the single most important rule in Part C.*

**`P-C2` — Subversion is offered only as addition, never as replacement.**
`A3-C40`, `A3-C09`. Permitted form: **"Want to try a version where…?"** Forbidden forms: "instead of…", "a more original approach…", "this is a common choice, have you considered…", "many writers avoid…". **Confidence: high.**

**`P-C3` — A subversion offer is always about a *new* artifact, never an edit to the existing one.**
`A3-C40`; module `04`'s **zero auto-inserted text** rule. Accepting a subversion offer creates a *variant* the child can keep, discard, or ignore. It never modifies the character they already made. **Confidence: high.**

**`P-C4` — Subversion offers are rate-limited hard and are the lowest-priority offer class.**
Module `04` E.3.1 (3 unrequested offers per character, 5 per session, never two in a row). **DESIGN DECISION: at most 1 subversion offer per character, and only after the child has produced substantive content on their own.** A subversion offer to a blank character is a directive. **Confidence: moderate-high.**

**`P-C5` — Subversion ladder by band.**
`A3-C44`, `A3-C40`, module `01`'s band structure. These are **defaults, not gates** (shared conventions §8.1) — a B2 child who asks for a B5-style prompt gets it, and observed output overrides band (§8.2).

| Band | Subversion class that is reliably reachable | Form |
|---|---|---|
| **B1 (5–6, K–1)** | **None offered by default.** `A3-C14`/`R2.1s`: genre adaptation is nascent. Available only on request. | — |
| **B2 (7–8, Gr 2–3)** | **Single swap.** One element changes; everything else holds. | "What if the [X] was really small / really silly / on the other side?" |
| **B3 (9–10, Gr 4–5)** | **Role swap and motive addition.** `A3-C08`, `A3-C44`. | "What if the helper is the main character?" · "What if the villain has a reason?" |
| **B4 (11–12, Gr 6–7)** | **Consequence and cost.** The subversion has downstream effects. | "What if the happy ending causes the next problem?" |
| **B5 (13–14, Gr 8–9)** | **Framing and perspective.** Who is telling this, and why this way. `A3-C38` | "Who benefits from this story being told this way?" |

**Confidence: moderate.** The band assignments are **DESIGN DECISION** inferences from `A3-C44` (moral ambiguity by 6–8) and the module `01` progression; the *ladder ordering* (swap → role → motive → consequence → framing) is not itself a retrieved finding.

**`P-C6` — Metafictive subversion is legitimate earlier than "sophistication" framing implies.**
`A3-C19`. Prompts like "what if your character knows they're in a story?" are supported from **B2**, on the retrieved finding that young children engage with the metafictive. **Confidence: moderate.**

**`P-C7` — No appearance-to-goodness mapping anywhere in the product.**
`A3-C45`. Binding on illustration sets, avatar options, example characters, and any generated description. Concretely: villain examples are not disproportionately dark-coloured, scarred, disfigured, or fat; hero examples are not disproportionately conventionally attractive. If the tool offers visual traits, they carry no moral valence in the tool's own materials. **The tool never comments on a child's own appearance-morality pairing.** **Confidence: high on the rule; the underlying claim is a single `[E1]` study with no sample size retrieved.**

**`P-C8` — Moral complexity is an offer with a hard "no thanks" and no repetition.**
`A3-C44`, `A3-C46`, `A3-C24`, `A3-C31`. A pure-evil villain is a legitimate, genre-conventional, developmentally-normal character. The tool may offer "does your villain want anything?" **once**; if declined, it does not return to it for that character. **Confidence: high.**

**`P-C9` — Add to the union do-not-flag list (shared conventions §11.3).**
The following must never be flagged, scored, counted, reported to an educator, or commented on:

| Never flag | Why |
|---|---|
| A flat character | `A3-C24`, `A3-C31`; module `01` `A1-C47`, `A1-C52` |
| A purely good hero or purely evil villain | `A3-C24`, `A3-C31`, `A3-C46` |
| A chosen-one / prophecy / orphan / dark-lord character | `A3-C07`, `P-C1` |
| A character strongly resembling one from a book, show or game | `A3-C15`, `A3-C16` |
| Absence of any OPTIONAL TROPE from any Part B table | `P-B1`, `P-B2` |
| A story that doesn't match its selected genre | `P-A1`, `P-B10` |
| Two or more genres selected at once | `A3-C18`, `P-B10` |
| A fable with no stated moral, a mystery with no solution, a fantasy with no magic | `P-B2` |
| Magic with no rules or cost | `A3-C22` |
| Technology with no explanation | `A3-C29`, `A3-C30` |
| A character who does not change | `A3-C24` |

**Confidence: high.**

# PART D — Spooky and horror: developmentally appropriate scary content

**Why this gets its own part.** It carries the module's highest product risk, and — unusually for this subject area — it has a real empirical literature behind it. It is also the area where the gap between **what is empirically established** and **what is professional practice** matters most, so each claim below is explicit about which it is.

## D.1 — WHAT RESEARCH SAYS: what frightens children, and how that changes

**`A3-C48`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **The central empirical finding: what frightens children shifts from the fantastic to the realistic with age.** Retrieved, associated with Joanne Cantor's programme (including *Children's Fear Responses to Mass Media: Testing Some Piagetian Predictions*, *Journal of Communication*, 1984, and *Children's fright reactions to television and films*): **"Younger children are more likely than their older counterparts to respond with fear to blatantly fantastic happenings, and less likely to respond to threats communicated in a more abstract manner"**; "the percentage of children being frightened by news **increased from kindergarten to the elementary school years**, whereas the tendency to be frightened by **fantastic, unreal content showed a decreasing trend**"; "as children grow older, the TV shows and movies that frighten them most are those that depict **more realistic threats.**"
*Retrieved via:* [Wiley, Cantor 1984, *Journal of Communication*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1984.tb02162.x), [ScienceDirect, Children's fright reactions to television and films](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0304422X9400012U), [Wiley, Cantor, Fright Responses to Media (reference work)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781118783764.wbieme0165), [Encyclopedia.com, Fear and the Media](https://www.encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/fear-and-media).

**`A3-C49`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Perceptual salience dominates in younger children; conceptual threat dominates in older ones.** Retrieved: "young children react to televised stimuli predominantly in terms of their **perceptible characteristics**, while older children tend to respond more to **conceptual components** of the televised stimulus, such as the perpetrator's motives or potential future threats"; "**proximity of the story had a significant impact on older children's responses but not those of younger children**"; "younger children are more likely than older children to attend to striking visual cues." From a practitioner-facing summary of the same literature: preschoolers are "most frightened by things that look scary"; at **6–8 years** children "are still most frightened by overtly scary images, but they're starting to become aware of the difference between fantasy and reality."
*Retrieved via:* [ScienceDirect, Children's fright reactions to television and films](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0304422X9400012U), [MediaSmarts, Dealing with fear and media](https://mediasmarts.ca/teacher-resources/dealing-fear-and-media), [Education Week, What Scares Children?](https://www.edweek.org/education/what-scares-children/1996/04).
**Age mapping:** the retrieved 6–8 band maps to **B1–B2**. The literature is **media-reception research (television and film)**, not writing research. See the transfer caveat in D.4.

**`A3-C50`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Fright reactions to media can be intense and durable — with retrieved numbers that need care.** Retrieved: Cantor "collected **more than 500 class papers**" written by students about their own fright reactions; a described review covered **530 papers written by students over a three-year period (1997–2000)**. Reported: **"46% of these students said that the movie had caused nightmares or other sleep disturbances, and 75% reported that it had influenced some area of their waking life."** Separately, from work retrieved as **Harrison and Cantor**: "**one third of the students who reported having been frightened said that the fear effects had lasted more than a year.**" Retrieved general characterization: fright reactions "can often be intense and long-lasting, involving sleep problems, emotional disturbances, and long-term changes in attitudes and behavior."
*Retrieved via:* [SAGE Encyclopedia of Children, Adolescents, and the Media — Fear Reactions](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/childmedia/chpt/fear-reactions), [ResearchGate, Tales from the Screen: Enduring Fright Reactions to Scary Media](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/252012246_Tales_from_the_Screen_Enduring_Fright_Reactions_to_Scary_Media), [University of Michigan Record, scary movies have lasting effects](https://record.umich.edu/articles/scary-movies-can-have-lasting-effects-on-children-and-teens-study-says).
> **⚠️ Number handling.** "More than 500" and "530" are two framings of what may or may not be the same corpus; I could not confirm. **These are retrospective self-report studies of college students recalling childhood**, which is a design with known recall and selection limitations — retrieved sources do not characterise those limitations, and I am flagging the design, not a stated critique. **Do not cite 46% / 75% / one-third externally.**

**`A3-C51`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Most children seek out scary experiences, and do so often.** Retrieved description of *Recreational Fear Across Childhood: A Cross-Sectional Study of Scary Activities that Children Enjoy* (Taranu, Clasen, Rosas, Dodd & Andersen; PubMed 40522538): **a survey of 1,600 parents/caregivers (67% female) of children aged 1–17 years (50% female)**, covering **19 activity categories**. Reported: **93% of children were reported to enjoy at least one recreational fear activity**; "**seven out of ten children sought it out weekly, and one in five looked for it daily**"; "distinct age-related trends in engagement were found for different activity types"; activities involving **height and speed** (swings, slides, climbing) were most popular, "with scary stories from movies, video games, and books close behind."
*Retrieved via:* [PubMed 40522538](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40522538/), [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/the-psychology-of-scary-fun-new-study-reveals-nearly-all-children-enjoy-recreational-fear/), [Aarhus University Recreational Fear Lab publications](https://cc.au.dk/en/recreational-fear-lab/publications), [Sussex figshare record](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Recreational_fear_across_childhood_A_cross-sectional_study_of_scary_activities_that_children_enjoy/29626052/1/files/56487485.pdf).
> **⚠️ Possible conflict, documented not resolved.** A separate Aarhus-affiliated retrieval states "**over 90% of Danish children aged 1 to 17** find joy in experiences that scare them." The study description above gives 93% from a 1,600-parent survey without stating nationality. These may be the same result described twice, or two different samples. **I cannot tell, and I am not picking one.** Note also this is **parent-report**, not child-report.

**`A3-C52`** `[E6]` `[SNIPPET]` **The claimed benefits of recreational fear are promising and not established.** Retrieved: the lab "has been exploring the positive effects of recreational fear, which **may** help reduce anxiety and improve emotional regulation"; "**early findings suggest** that frightening experiences might help us practice how to deal with scary situations and unpleasant emotions in a safe environment"; "high prevalence of recreational fear enjoyment among children is **linked to** lower anxiety and stronger coping mechanisms." Also retrieved, from practitioner sources: scary stories "help kids practice dealing with fear and build emotional resilience."
*Retrieved via:* [Aarhus University Recreational Fear Lab](https://cc.au.dk/en/recreational-fear-lab), [Discover Magazine, the science of recreational fear](https://www.discovermagazine.com/the-science-of-recreational-fear-why-we-love-horror-movies-and-other-spooky-thrills), [Child and Family Blog, Scary Play Can Help Children Learn](https://childandfamilyblog.com/scary-play-children-learning/), [ParentMap, Tales Dark and Grim](https://www.parentmap.com/article/tales-dark-and-grim-why-kids-love-and-need-scary-stories).
> **Tiered `[E6]` deliberately, and this matters.** The retrieved language is hedged ("may," "early findings suggest," "linked to") and the direction of causation is not established — children lower in anxiety may simply seek more recreational fear. **`A3-C51` (children seek scary content) is far better evidenced than `A3-C52` (it is good for them).** Character Studio may build on `A3-C51`. It may **not** market on `A3-C52`.

> **The two-sided evidence base, stated plainly:** `A3-C50` says media fright can be intense and durable. `A3-C51` says nearly all children seek scary experiences anyway. **Both are true.** A product that acts only on the first becomes uselessly timid; a product that acts only on the second is negligent. The boundaries in D.3 are an attempt to hold both.

## D.2 — WHAT RESEARCH SAYS: professional practice — where the empirical evidence runs out

Everything in D.2 is **`[E4]` professional practice**. It is what practitioners do. It is **not** evidence of what works.

**`A3-C53`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **Children's publishing has a described norm: real fear, guaranteed safety, resolved ending.** Retrieved from editorial and craft sources on middle-grade horror: "**Kids like being scared when they think they are safe at the same time, and books should always have a happy ending so children know everything will work out**"; horror is "an allegorical storytelling device that allows readers to process their fears safely"; "**kids face scary challenges in the real world, but in their stories those frights are encountered and ultimately left behind, with a measure of closure or at least the promise of one**"; and "**surprise is the secret sauce of middle-grade horror.**" Retrieved counter-note: children's authors "should not assume that writing for kids means simpler storytelling rules apply."
*Retrieved via:* [Writer's Digest, 7 tips on how to write middle grade horror](https://www.writersdigest.com/whats-new/how-to-write-middle-grade-horror-7-tips), [Writer and the Wolf, Club Ed Conversations: Editing Horror, MG and YA](https://www.writerandthewolf.com/2023/07/31/club-ed-conversations-editing-horror-middle-grade-and-ya/), [Darker Times, Setting the Record Straight About Middle Grade Horror](https://www.darkertimes.com/middle-grade-horror-russell/).

**`A3-C54`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **Suspense and graphic threat are treated as different instruments, and children's-literature guidance prefers the former.** Retrieved: "**fear and suspense are rooted in anxiety — the expectation that something bad will happen**"; "psychological horror capitalizes on fear of the unknown and internal struggles, whereas gore relies on explicit, graphic content"; "**a book should use narrative to describe violence that's happened off-stage rather than dragging the reader through the violent action on stage**"; "readers will usually imagine something far nastier than whatever you were about to spell out anyway"; "**sometimes a closed door is scarier than the creature behind it**." Retrieved children's-specific guidance: an author "should only include what's absolutely required, and should soften its shock value when possible," letting "plot twists and irony… drive a story's suspense, while minimizing the shock and horror impact of violent or dark scenes." Corroborating from a library source on graphic-novel horror: "**it's not the monster hiding behind the door that terrifies people, but what they conjure in their mind the moment before that door opens.**"
*Retrieved via:* [Julie Hahnke, Evaluating Violence in Children's Literature](https://juliehahnke.com/evaluating-violence-in-childrens-literature/), [Horror Tree, The Elements of Horror](https://horrortree.com/the-elements-of-horror-building-suspense-and-tension-in-your-writing/), [editing.services, Whispered Fears: The Art of Writing Quiet Horror](https://editing.services/blog/whispered-fears-the-art-of-writing-quiet-horror), [SLJ, Be Afraid (but not too afraid)](https://www.slj.com/story/be-afraid-but-not-too-afraid-graphic-novel-horror-for-middle-grade-readers).

> **This is the technical heart of Part D.** *Anticipation is the genre's engine; depiction is optional decoration.* That is not a compromise imposed for child safety — it is what the craft sources say makes horror **work better**. This means a spooky mode with strict content boundaries is not a watered-down spooky mode.

**`A3-C55`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **Librarians describe scariness as highly individual, adults as systematically over-cautious, and children as self-regulating.** Retrieved: "**creepiness is very subjective — a book that pleasantly spooks one first-grader may horrify another**"; "there's a range of tolerance among readers when it comes to scary books, and **adults often skew to the conservative side** when evaluating and recommending titles for children with a fright factor"; "**students typically indicate when something is disturbing, as they're testing their own preferences and limits, and will usually stop reading and return a book if it's too scary.**" From a library-journal source on middle-grade readers: "**middle grade readers are exploring independently what their horror tolerance is and where they can personally draw the line**," and "many have put down horror that was just too much for them."
*Retrieved via:* [NYPL, Spooky Books for Kids](https://www.nypl.org/blog/2018/10/24/kids-halloween), [Ms. Nancy's Library blog, scary and not-so-scary books for elementary students](http://msnancy.org/2013/05/13/scary-and-not-so-scary-books-for-elementary-students-from-your-friendly-librarian/), [SLJ, Be Afraid (but not too afraid)](https://www.slj.com/story/be-afraid-but-not-too-afraid-graphic-novel-horror-for-middle-grade-readers), [CCBC-Net archive discussion](https://labweb.education.wisc.edu/ccbc-net/2014-10/20084.html).
> **The child-agency finding here is the strongest argument in Part D for an exit control** that the child operates, rather than a filter the tool operates. See `P-D3`.

**`A3-C56`** `[E5]` `[SNIPPET]` **A widely-used commercial rating framework describes scary content by age.** Retrieved from Common Sense Media's own rating documentation: **ages 0–2** — "children this age should not be exposed to any violent or aggressive content" and "should not be exposed to scary or emotionally intense media"; "darkness, strange sounds, distorted faces or bodies, and other grotesque images can all be upsetting." **Ages 8–9** — "video game violence that involves first-person shooting and blood isn't age-appropriate — nor is movie or TV violence that includes lots of blood"; "kids might start seeking out scary content looking for thrills, but **they can still be frightened**." **Ages 10–12** — "kids often seek out scary/horror content looking for thrills, **particularly when watching as a group**, but they can still be frightened and have bad dreams"; "more realistic and more interactive forms of media violence… may have the most negative effects." *Retrieved via:* [Common Sense Media, How We Rate: 0–2](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/about-us/our-mission/about-our-ratings/0-2), [How We Rate: 8–9](https://d8.commonsensemedia.org/about-us/our-mission/about-our-ratings/8-9), [How We Rate: 10–12](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/about-us/our-mission/about-our-ratings/10-12), [How We Rate: Books](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/about-us/our-mission/about-our-ratings/books).

Note also it rates **consumption**, not composition.

**`A3-C57`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **Educators are advised to separate "violence in writing" from "violent writing," and to protect creative expression.** Retrieved: "it is essential that schools and writing teachers clarify the **distinction between violent writing and violence in writing** — and educate students on that difference"; "**often, student writers include violence in their stories as an attempt to understand their complicated feelings about the brutality they see or experience, and students' right to creative expression can and should be protected**"; intervention "should be recommended only when there is genuine and deep concern that the writing is more of a call for help or threat than a literary creation"; and "**teachers shouldn't be asked to make psychological judgments that even trained psychologists find difficult to make.**" Retrieved school-policy note: "all material should be age appropriate; students should be made aware that their stories should also be appropriate for school."

## D.3 — WHAT CHARACTER STUDIO SHOULD DO: spooky/horror content boundaries

### D.3.1 — The design position

`A3-C51` (nearly all children seek scary content) + `A3-C54` (anticipation, not depiction, is the genre's engine) + `A3-C53` (safety and resolution are the children's-publishing norm) ⇒ **Character Studio supports a genuinely spooky mode, and constrains depiction rather than dread.**

### D.3.2 — Boundary table by band

The move from B2 to B5 is **fantastic-and-visible → ambiguous-and-psychological**, *not* fantastic → realistic. A tool that "matures" its spooky suggestions toward realistic peril is walking directly into the retrieved finding.

### D.3.3 — Content boundaries that hold at every band

Never generated, suggested, or illustrated by Character Studio, at any age:

| Absolute boundary | Basis |
|---|---|
| Gore, injury description, physical suffering | `A3-C54` (off-page is the craft norm), `A3-C56` |
| Death of a child character as a tool suggestion | `A3-C53` (safety norm), **DESIGN DECISION** |
| Harm framed as deserved or funny | `A3-C42`, `A3-C45` |
| Self-harm or suicide content in any form | Out of this module's competence — module `05` and qualified review govern |

### D.3.4 — Product rules

**`P-D1` — Ship a spooky mode, and make it actually spooky.**
`A3-C51`, `A3-C54`. Suppressing spookiness entirely misreads the evidence: the seeking is near-universal, and the craft sources say the good version doesn't need the content that worries adults. **Confidence: high.**

**`P-D2` — Constrain depiction; never constrain dread.**
`A3-C54`. The tool's own suggestions favour anticipation, implication, the unseen, and the closed door. It does not suggest what is behind the door. **Confidence: high** on the rule; `[E4]` on the underlying craft claim.

**`P-D3` — The child holds the intensity control, and it is always visible.**
`A3-C55` (children self-regulate and are testing their own limits), `A3-C56` ("what's scary for one kid is another kid's biggest thrill"). A persistent, one-tap **"less spooky"** control, plus an instant exit from spooky mode that **preserves everything the child wrote.** Never asks why. Never reported to anyone. **Confidence: high.** *This is the single highest-value spooky feature and it is better evidenced than any tool-side filter.*

**`P-D4` — Default gently, and let the child move up, not the calendar.**
`A3-C55`, shared conventions §8. Band sets the starting point in D.3.2; the child's use of the intensity control moves it. **No age gate.** **Confidence: high.**

**`P-D5` — Offer resolution; never require it.**
`A3-C53`. The editorial norm is real and worth surfacing as an option ("do they get out of it?"). It is a *convention*, and it goes in the CONVENTION column — which per `P-B1` means it may be offered and per `P-B2` its absence may never be flagged. **Confidence: moderate-high.**

**`P-D6` — Never escalate toward realism as the child ages.**
`A3-C48`. See D.3.2 note. **Confidence: high** — this is the rule most directly derived from an `[E1]` finding in Part D.

**`P-D7` — Spooky and mystery share a no-crime default.**
`A3-C25`, `P-B6`, D.3.3. **Confidence: high.**

**`P-D8` — Realistic fiction and spooky mode never combine into realistic peril in tool suggestions.**
`A3-C28`, `A3-C48`. The child may write it. The tool does not propose it. Hybrid selection of realistic + spooky produces **atmosphere** suggestions (the house feels wrong; someone isn't acting like themselves), never **threat** suggestions. **Confidence: moderate-high.**

**`P-D9` — No psychological inference from creative writing. Ever.**
`A3-C57`, and module `05`'s exclusion of inferred `mood`, `sentiment`, `themes_detected`. Character Studio must not classify a child's story as concerning, must not compute a distress signal, must not surface "this student wrote about violence" to an educator, and must not alter its tone toward a child on the basis of dark content. Retrieved guidance is explicit that even **trained psychologists** find these judgments difficult. Genuine imminent-harm signals are a **platform trust-and-safety pathway with human review and qualified legal/clinical oversight**, wholly outside this module's creative-feedback surface. **Confidence: high. This rule is non-negotiable and should be treated as a launch blocker if violated.**

**`P-D10` — Never claim scary content is good for children.**
`A3-C52` (`[E6]`, hedged, causally ambiguous), `A3-C39` (Bettelheim contested). Character Studio may say children enjoy this (`A3-C51`, well evidenced). It may not say it builds resilience, reduces anxiety, or helps them process fear. **Confidence: high.**

## E.2 — Core decision logic

```
# ─── GENRE SELECTION ────────────────────────────────────────────────
IF child has not chosen a genre
THEN offer no genre-derived prompts at all
AND  the character-creation experience is complete and valid as-is
     (P-A2; shared conventions §11.2)

IF child chooses ≥1 genre
THEN genre ∈ multiset — multi-select is native, not an edge case   (P-B10)
AND  prompt_pool = UNION over chosen genres of CONVENTION prompts   (E.1)
AND  dedupe near-identical prompts across genres
AND  NEVER warn, rank, or ask the child to pick a primary genre

IF child changes or clears genre
THEN nothing already written is discarded, greyed, or re-validated  (P-A2)

IF band == B1 (K–1)
THEN genre-derived prompts are OFF by default                       (P-A3, A3-C14)
AND  remain reachable if the child asks — this is not a gate        (§8.1)

# ─── THE PROHIBITIONS (checked before any offer is rendered) ────────
ASSERT no code path computes genre_conformance, trope_count,
       originality, cliché_flag, completeness, or archetype_label
       (P-A4, P-B2, P-C1; module 05 data-model exclusions)

ASSERT no offer is generated because something is ABSENT             (P-B2)
       # Offers are generated from what the child HAS written, never
       # from a diff against a genre template. This is the single
       # most important implementation detail in this module.

# ─── OFFER GATING ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
FUNCTION may_offer(prompt, state):
    IF state.unrequested_offers_this_character >= 3   RETURN false  # module 04 E.3.1
    IF state.unrequested_offers_this_session   >= 5   RETURN false  # module 04 E.3.1
    IF state.child_content_since_last_offer == 0      RETURN false  # module 04 E.3.1
    IF prompt.genre_worldbuilding
       AND state.worldbuilding_offers[genre] >= cap(genre)  RETURN false  # P-B4
    IF prompt.class == SUBVERSION
       AND (state.subversion_offers_this_character >= 1
            OR state.child_substantive_content == false)   RETURN false  # P-C4
    IF prompt.class == ROUNDNESS
       AND genre ∈ {fable, folktale, fairy_tale, adventure} RETURN false  # P-B3
    IF prompt.class == MORAL_COMPLEXITY
       AND state.moral_complexity_declined_for_character   RETURN false  # P-C8
    RETURN true

# ─── SUBVERSION ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
IF may_offer(subversion, state)
THEN phrase it as "Want to try a version where …?"                  (P-C2)
AND  select rung by band from P-C5, overridden upward by observed output (§8.2)
AND  accepting creates a SEPARATE VARIANT; the original is untouched (P-C3)
AND  declining is silent, costless, and never re-asked for this character

IF the source material is a named living tradition
THEN no subversion offer, at any band                               (P-B7c)

# ─── SPOOKY ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
IF genre includes spooky
THEN suggestion set := D.3.2[band] ∩ NOT D.3.3
AND  intensity control is rendered persistently and is child-operated (P-D3)
AND  intensity NEVER escalates toward realism with band              (P-D6, A3-C48)

IF genre includes spooky AND genre includes realistic
THEN suggestions are ATMOSPHERIC only, never THREAT                  (P-D8)

IF child writes content beyond the band defaults
THEN do nothing. No block, no warning, no softening, no flag,
     no change of tone, no educator notification.                    (P-D9, A3-C57)
     # Trust-and-safety escalation is a separate, human-reviewed
     # platform pathway and is NOT part of the creative surface.
```

## E.3 — Convention-vs-trope handling: the three rules that must not be violated

## E.5 — Cliché handling, stated as a policy

1. **Character Studio does not detect clichés.** (`P-C1`)
2. **Character Studio does not have an originality objective.** No metric, no nudge, no ranking.
3. **A common choice receives exactly the same response as a rare one.** Same tone, same length, same enthusiasm.
4. **Originality is available only as an additive invitation, only via the subversion path, only rate-limited, only after the child has produced their own content.** (`P-C2`, `P-C3`, `P-C4`)
5. **Rationale to record in the codebase, honestly:** the developmental-normality-of-clichés premise is **`[UNVERIFIED]`** at the B3–B5 range this product mostly serves (see `A3-C16`). This policy is adopted because (a) the shared default under uncertainty is neutrality (§9), (b) "overused" is a property of adult reading history rather than child invention (`A3-C07`), (c) borrowing is documented and under-exploited rather than pathological (`A3-C15`), and (d) the cost asymmetry is severe — wrongly telling a child their idea is unoriginal is a much worse error than failing to broaden a child who was content.

# PART F — Cross-module tensions

Named rather than papered over, per instruction. Each states the conflict, the proposed resolution, and who should decide.

### F.1 — Genre scaffolds vs. module `04`'s offer budget · **Resolved, no conflict**

Module `04` caps unrequested offers at 3 per character / 5 per session and permits **zero** auto-inserted text. A genre feature naturally wants to introduce several prompts at once. **Resolution: module `04` wins outright.** Genre prompts consume the existing budget rather than extending it (E.4). Genre selection is a *filter on which prompts are eligible*, not a licence to offer more. **This module proposes no exception to the zero-auto-insert rule and none should ever be granted.**

### F.2 — Genre scaffolds vs. module `05`'s ban on archetype/trope classification · **Real tension, resolvable**

Module `05` excludes "any archetype/trope classification of the child's character" from the data model, on the grounds that it "encodes a canon." Part B of this module is, structurally, a canon of conventions and tropes.

**Resolution — the distinction is direction of application:**

| Permitted | Prohibited |
|---|---|
| Genre knowledge applied **outward**, to decide which optional prompts the child may see | Genre knowledge applied **inward**, to label what the child made |
| A stored field for *the genre the child chose* | Any stored field for *the archetype the tool inferred* |
| "Would you like a prompt about helpers?" | `character.archetype = "mentor"` |

`P-A4` and `P-B2` implement this. **Module `05` wins on every storage and analytics question.** If an implementation ever needs to classify the child's character to decide what to offer, that is evidence the offer logic is wrong — offers must derive from what the child wrote, not from a diff against a template (see the `ASSERT` in E.2).

### F.3 — Genre play at B1 vs. a genre picker in the UI · **Real tension, resolved toward module `01`**

Module `01` `R2.1s` says do not build genre/audience-adaptation features for B0–B1, because genre knowledge is only nascent (`A1-C107`). A genre picker in a shared K–8 UI will be seen by K–1 children.

**Resolution:** the picker exists but genre-derived prompting is **off by default below B2** (`P-A3`), and — critically — a K–1 child who picks "spooky" and writes something indistinguishable from their non-spooky writing **has not failed**. No differentiation is expected, measured, or flagged. **Module `01` wins on the default; shared conventions §8.1 wins on the gate (there is none).**

### F.4 — Module `02`'s structure recommender vs. this module's genre affinity · **Latent tension, flagged**

Module `02`'s `recommend_structures` scores structures partly by `genre_affinity`, and `S5-R3` requires that if the hero's journey appears, at least one non-conflict-centred structure appears at equal visual weight. Meanwhile `A3-C10` here documents the Eurocentrism critique of archetype systems, and `A3-C11` the imposition of Western categories generally.

**Proposed resolution:** `S5-R3`'s equal-weight rule should be **generalised from the hero's journey to every genre-driven structure recommendation.** A child selecting "adventure" should not be steered exclusively into conflict-centred Western shapes, since shared conventions §11.1 states there is no single correct story shape. **This is a recommendation to module `02`'s owner, not a change this module can make.**

### F.5 — Recreational-fear evidence vs. the platform's duty of care · **Unresolved; needs a human decision**

`A3-C51` says nearly all children seek scary content, and `A3-C55` says adults systematically over-restrict it. `A3-C50` says media fright can produce durable effects. `A3-C52`, the "it's good for them" claim, is `[E6]` and hedged.

## G.2 — Cultural assumptions — in the research, and in genre taxonomy itself

### In the research

- **Anglophone corpora throughout.** Propp used **Russian folktales** (`A3-C08`); Mendlesohn used a predominantly Anglophone corpus (`A3-C21`); the children's-literature genre definitions come from **US library and university guides**; the fair-play rules are **British and American, 1928–1929** (`A3-C26`); the fairy-tale conventions are **early-modern European** (`A3-C38`).
- **A named recreational-fear retrieval concerns Danish children** (`A3-C51`). Enjoyment norms around scary play are unlikely to be culturally invariant, and no cross-cultural comparison was retrieved.
- **The genre-preference survey data (`A3-C17`) measures choices inside an already gender-marketed publishing environment**, and its percentages could not be attributed to a specific document.
- **Archetype systems carry an explicit Eurocentrism critique** (`A3-C10`): "an Eurocentric and colonialist device to level the specifics of individual cultures and their stories in the service of grand abstraction."

### In the taxonomy itself — the assumption this module most wants flagged

**"Fantasy / adventure / mystery / realistic fiction / science fiction / fable / folktale / fairy tale / humor / horror" is a Western publishing and school-library taxonomy.** It is a shelving system that became a curriculum. It is not a description of how human storytelling divides.

Four specific consequences:

1. **"Folktale" is the worst offender.** It bins Jataka, Panchatantra, Anansi, Br'er Rabbit, La Llorona, and Chinese oral narrative into one category defined mainly by *not being modern Western authored fiction* (`A3-C34`–`A3-C37`). The Anansi case is the demonstration: a trope card records "clever spider tricks people"; the Asante account records a figure "who teaches moral, ethical, political, or social values based on his ability to lead a person to the truth," and the diasporic account records a figure of resistance under enslavement (`A3-C35`). **The card is not a summary of the tradition. It is a different thing wearing its name.**
2. **Some traditions are not "content" at all.** `A3-C12` documents restrictions "based on seasonality, occasion, topic, teller, audience," sacred stories with restricted tellers, and initiation requirements — corroborated independently by module `02`'s `A2-C80`. **A genre picker structurally cannot represent a story you may only tell in winter, or only if you have been recognised as a teller.** This is why `P-B7a` exists.
3. **"Fairy tale" carries a specific class-and-gender project.** `A3-C38`: literary fairy tales established their conventions "altered to address a reading public formed by the aristocracy and the middle classes." The princess/stepmother/marriage package is a historical artefact, and `P-B8` keeps the tool from proposing it.
4. **Every product rule in this module is therefore doubly contingent** — on Western genre categories being a useful frame at all, and on the specific `[E4]` descriptions of them being accurate.

### Bishop's frame applied to the tool itself

`A3-C13`: books are mirrors, windows, and sliding glass doors. A character-creation tool's **example set, default imagery, name suggestions and setting prompts are the mirrors and windows it provides.** A tool whose fantasy examples are uniformly castle-and-sword, whose realistic-fiction examples are uniformly suburban, and whose folktales are uniformly Grimm, is teaching a canon while claiming to offer a blank page. **Example-set composition is a cultural decision, not an art-direction decision.**

## G.3 — Accessibility considerations

**`A3-C58`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Autistic children's engagement rises with interest-aligned content, and rule-governed genres are described as a natural fit.** Retrieved: "students have been shown to **write better when writing about their special interest** compared to a control topic"; literacy tasks can be engaged "by framing them around their special interests." Retrieved on genre specifically: "science fiction and fantasy worlds are often built on **internally consistent systems with clear and logical rules** that appeal to autistic people who seek order, logic, and predictability," and "many science fiction and fantasy plots follow **recognizable patterns** that can be appealing." Retrieved neuroimaging note: "when comparing personalized interest narratives to generic narratives, **stronger activation and more inter-subject consistency in language responses were found for personalized narratives.**" Retrieved on play: autistic children's imaginative play "may look different — more solitary, more repetitive, and more tied to specific narratives or characters they're intensely interested in."
*Retrieved via:* [SAGE, parent-reported fiction vs non-fiction book preferences of school-age children with ASD](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2396941518806109), [bioRxiv, incorporating special interests to investigate the language system in autism](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.04.04.647117.full.pdf), [PMC, how autistic and neurotypical children's interests influence novel word learning](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11362225/), [Forbrain, autism and reading](https://www.forbrain.com/autism-learning/reading/).
> **Product reading — and note the inversion.** `A3-C22` treats "magic needs rules" as a craft opinion the tool must not impose. `A3-C58` says rule-governed worlds are exactly what some autistic children find engaging. **Both are honoured by the same design: rule-building is available and enthusiastically supported when the child reaches for it, and never proposed as a standard.** Repetition and returning to the same character or scenario is documented engagement, **not perseveration to be redirected** — the tool must not nudge toward variety.

**`A3-C59`** `[E1]` `[SNIPPET]` **Children with developmental language disorder show weaker narrative macrostructure, and narrative intervention has evidence behind it.** Retrieved: "narrative skills are impaired in children with developmental language disorder (DLD)"; "**children with DLD performed weaker in six out of seven story grammar elements**" compared to typically developing peers; "typically developing children scored higher than the developmental language disorder group on macrostructure total scores." Retrieved on intervention: narrative language intervention shows "improved narrative structure and complexity in addition to improved receptive and expressive use of syntax, morphology and general language use"; **Story Champs** is named as an evidence-based narrative intervention targeting "both narrative macrostructure and microstructure."
*Retrieved via:* [Frontiers in Education, Narrative skills of children with DLD: retelling in macrostructure](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1626433/full), [ASHA JSLHR, Investigating Narrative Performance in Children With DLD: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/2022_JSLHR-22-00017), [ScienceDirect, pilot RCT of a narrative intervention program for children with DLD](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165587625000308).
> **Product reading — and a warning.** Genre conventions are *structural* expectations. A child with DLD may know a genre perfectly and still not produce its structure. **Any inference from "this story doesn't look like its genre" to anything about the child is invalid, and `P-B2` already forbids computing it.** The positive implication: genres with **low structural load** — fable (very short, 2–3 characters), realistic fiction (no worldbuilding), humor (a single incongruity) — are lower-barrier entry points, and should be **equally visible, never assigned.**

**`A3-C60`** `[E4]` `[SNIPPET]` **Culturally sustaining pedagogy treats children's home storytelling traditions as assets, and argues for widening what counts as legitimate composing.** Retrieved: culturally sustaining pedagogy "seeks to perpetuate and foster linguistic, literate, and cultural pluralism"; "emergent bilinguals continue to develop their heritage languages in their homes and communities, and **making space for these children's full linguistic repertoires can result in academic, cognitive, and social benefits**"; educators "should seek to **shift what constitutes legitimate composing** in traditional English literacy classrooms and welcome storytelling in diverse modes, including visual, linguistic, and audio representations"; all students benefit from opportunities "to share and respond playfully to multilingual texts, in the process becoming storytellers themselves."
*Retrieved via:* [UW Pressbooks, Culturally Sustaining Pedagogy (Supporting Multilingual Learners)](https://uw.pressbooks.pub/eedumll/chapter/2-2-culturally-sustaining-pedagogy/), [SAGE, Starting small: engaging young learners with literacy through multilingual storytelling](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14687984241303390), [MDPI *Education Sciences*, Writing Strategies for Elementary Multilingual Writers: A Systematic Review](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/14/7/759), [Wiley, Storytelling in a Bilingual Classroom Through the Lens of Epistemic Diversity and Translanguaging](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ijal.12714).
